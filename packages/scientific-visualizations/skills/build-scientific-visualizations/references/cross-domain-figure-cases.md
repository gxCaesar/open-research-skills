# Cross-domain figure cases

Read alongside `domain-visualization-selection.md` when transferring a design into
single-cell, genomic, protein or clinical work. These are selected primary-figure
readings, not whole-paper reviews, current benchmark endorsements or journal rules.
Observed describes the cited figure and legend. Transfer is a design inference;
apply it only when the new data support the same relationship. Do not copy source art.

## Single-cell perturbation: define the held-out entity beside the score

Source: [GEARS](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-023-01905-6), Fig. 2a–h
and its legend; Results, single-gene and multigene prediction sections.

**Observed.** Panels a and e illustrate different held-out perturbation categories;
b and f place their performance comparisons nearby. A wider gene-level example in
g complements population summaries. The legend distinguishes top-20 differential
gene metrics from correlation across all genes, and defines its intervals over five
trained models with different data splits.

**Transfer.** Reserve adjacent space for the split diagram and the comparison it
defines. Use the same category order in both. Give a many-gene example enough width
for identities, while retaining the population comparison. Name each metric's gene
population instead of using one blanket label for the page.

**Boundary.** The reported split variability is not additional biological replication.
The attractive example is not evidence of transfer to another cell type. Reuse the
layout relationship, not the historical baseline lineup or superiority claim.

## Genomics: align evidence layers without equating their status

Source: [Enformer](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-021-01252-x), Fig. 2a–b
and its legend; Results, enhancer-prioritization section.

**Observed.** A wide HNRNPA1 locus aligns predicted expression, measured H3K27ac,
candidate/CRISPRi-validated enhancers, attention and gradient-based contributions.
Below it, distance-stratified prioritization comparisons show positive and negative
counts. Their legend identifies resampling and class-imbalance differences.

**Transfer.** Allocate width to the shared genomic coordinate, not separate boxed
tracks. Name measured, predicted and derived layers directly. Follow the locus with
the population-level test that answers whether the visually highlighted regions are
useful. Keep class counts near precision–recall comparisons when prevalence differs.

**Boundary.** Aligned peaks do not turn attention into causal regulation. Preserve
genome build, interval, strand and track scale from the new data; source coordinates
are not a reusable template. A selected locus cannot establish population accuracy.

## Protein design: couple the same candidate's structure and assay

Source: [RFdiffusion](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06415-8),
Fig. 6a–h, publisher PDF p. 10, legend and adjacent binder-validation text.

**Observed.** A design sequence introduces the page. Target-specific structure views
sit above their BLI titrations. The HA example continues into cryo-EM class averages,
reconstruction and structural superposition. The legend distinguishes design models,
AF2 predictions and experimental structures; the text limits specificity claims.

**Transfer.** Keep candidate identity and target encoding constant across coordinate
views and assay curves. Use a local structural enlargement for the interface and
different view angles only when they reveal additional geometry. Allocate space to
concentrations, time and fit context, not only the protein rendering.

**Boundary.** Structural agreement and measured binding answer different questions.
Neither proves proteome-wide specificity. Render new structures from exact coordinates;
do not use generated molecular illustrations as measured geometry. Historical success
rates are not a current benchmark or proof of matched computational budgets.

## Clinical resources: show linkage before proposing analysis

Source: [MIMIC-IV](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01899-x),
Fig. 2, publisher PDF p. 3, legend; Methods introduction and data-description sections.

**Observed.** A compact module diagram groups hospital, ICU and note content and
connects additional modalities. Its legend names linkage identifiers and deidentified
time. The accompanying text distinguishes patient, hospitalization and ICU records.
Access described in the paper requires training and a data-use agreement.

**Transfer.** Use a resource diagram to explain what can join and at which unit.
For a study-specific clinical analysis, add a separate reconciled eligibility flow
and patient-level split; a database schema cannot supply those counts. Distinguish
linkable modalities from the subset actually overlapping in the proposed cohort.

**Boundary.** Diagram arrows describe data relationships, not treatment effects.
Do not imply all modules coexist for every patient, treat admissions as independent
patients, or infer unrestricted access from the article title. Check the selected
release's current access conditions before acquiring data.

## Transfer check

State the relationship borrowed from one source, the new artifact that supports it,
and the important mismatch. If the mismatch changes the interpretation, redesign
the comparison rather than preserving the source page's visual resemblance.
